We have sat in a lot of product teams' standups. After enough of them, the thing you stop noticing is the product, and the thing you start noticing is the decision.

Not the quality of the decision — the shape of it. Who gets asked. How long it takes. What has to be true before anyone writes code. Teams building completely different products, in completely different markets, turn out to make decisions in a small number of recognizable ways. And the way a team decides predicts what it will be good at and where it will quietly bleed time.

Five patterns come up again and again.

## None of these is the bad one

That is the part worth saying out loud, because every list like this reads as a ranking and this one is not.

Each of these exists for a reason, and the reason is usually that it worked. A team becomes Sales-Driven because saying yes to customers closed the round. A team becomes Engineering-Led because the thing they built had to survive real load and it did. A team becomes a Committee because something shipped badly once and the review got added. These are scar tissue, and scar tissue is evidence that the wound healed.

The cost shows up later, when the thing that made the team successful at 20 people is the thing making it slow at 120. The Feature Factory that shipped its way to product-market fit now has a product nobody can explain in one sentence. The Committee that stopped the bad launch now cannot start the good one.

## What it costs, in actual money

The expensive part is rarely the feature you got wrong. It is the work that got built, then unbuilt.

Rework is the honest measure here: the share of a team’s time that goes into redoing something already done. Teams we talk to put it somewhere between 10% and 40%, and the ones at the high end are usually surprised to hear themselves say it. Multiply that share by a loaded cost per person and the number stops being a process complaint and starts being a line item. A team of 25 at 25% rework is burning well over a million dollars a year rebuilding things.

That arithmetic is also why the pattern matters more than the individual decision. A single bad call costs you a sprint. A decision-making habit costs you a share of every sprint, forever, until something changes it.

## Which one is yours

Most people reading this already have a guess. The guess is worth testing, for two reasons.

The first is that teams are rarely one pattern cleanly. The common result is a primary with a streak of something else — a Committee that turns into a Firefighter the week before a release, an Engineering-Led team with a Sales-Driven streak that nobody has named yet. The streak is usually where the friction is, because the two halves want different things.

The second is that the pattern you are in is hard to see from inside it. Every one of these feels like common sense when you are living it. "We check with everyone because that is how you avoid mistakes" is a true sentence and also a description of a bottleneck.
