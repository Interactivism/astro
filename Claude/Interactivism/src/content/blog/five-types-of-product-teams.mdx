We have sat in a lot of product teams' planning sessions. What stands out over time is the decision-making culture.

<Aside>
  Watch how a team makes decisions, and you can predict what they'll be good at, and where they'll quietly pay for it.
</Aside>

It's not about the quality of their decisions, but how they make them. Who is asked. How long it takes to get an answer. How disagreements are resolved. What has to be true before anyone writes code. Teams building different products in different markets end up making decisions in a small number of recognizable ways.

## Each archetype exists for a reason

Culture is born from lived experience.

A team becomes Sales-Driven because saying yes to customers closed a round. A team becomes Engineering-Led because the software they build simply can't fail under real load, and so far it hasn't. A team becomes a Committee because something shipped that shouldn't, and they added more reviews. This is scar tissue: proof the wound healed, and the reason the team moves stiffer now.

## What it costs

The cost shows up later, when what made the team successful at 10 people makes it slow at 100. The Feature Factory that shipped its way to product-market fit now has a product nobody can explain in one sentence. The Committee that stopped a bad launch now takes too long to ship a good one.

When a team ships the wrong thing, or ships too slowly, the bill comes in several forms:

* **Rework cost:** Time spent redoing what's already been done.
* **Opportunity cost:** The right thing ships late, and the value it would have created in the meantime is gone.
* **Poor user experience:** People live with the version that missed or shipped late, and they learn to expect less from your next release.
* **Market share loss:** Over time, the first 3 add up to customers who leave or never arrive.

The last 3 are real, but they're harder to quantify. Rework isn't, so start there.

Rework is the share of a team's time spent redoing something that should have been done already. Teams we talk to put it somewhere between 10% and 40%, and the ones at the high end are usually surprised to hear themselves say it. Multiply that share by a loaded cost per team member, and it stops being a process complaint and becomes a real line item: 25 people × 25% rework × $180k loaded cost comes to about $1.1M a year spent rebuilding things.

Rework is the cost you can act on first. It comes straight out of how a team decides, so changing a decision-making culture shows up in weeks, not the next fiscal year.

It's also why the pattern matters more than any single decision. A bad call costs you a sprint. A bad habit costs you a share of every sprint, forever, until something changes it.

## Which one is yours?

Most people reading this already have an idea of where they will land. That assumption is worth testing, for 2 reasons.

1. **Teams are rarely one clean type.** The common result is a primary type with a streak of something else — a Committee that turns into a Firefighter the week before a release, an Engineering-Led team with a Sales-Driven streak that nobody has named yet. And the streak is usually where the friction is, because the two parts want different things.
2. **The pattern you are in is hard to see from the inside.** Every one of these feels like common sense when you are living it. "We check with everyone because that is how we avoid mistakes" is a true sentence and also describes a bottleneck.

We built a short self-assessment: 12 questions about how your team actually operates, about 3 minutes to complete. You'll get your team archetype, an estimate of what rework is costing you, and three things you can change today that will move the needle tomorrow.
