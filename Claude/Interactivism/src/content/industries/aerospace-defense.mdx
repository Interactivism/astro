## Operators aren't users in the ordinary sense

Most software gets to assume an attentive person with time to explore. Ground systems and mission tooling don't. The operator is mid-shift, carrying context from the last handoff, and something just changed state.

What matters is what's legible in the first ten seconds: which alarm is the cause and which are its downstream echoes, what's affected, and what action is actually available right now. Everything else can be a click away. Designs that treat every piece of data as equally urgent push that triage work back onto the person — exactly when they have the least capacity for it.

## Modernization is a migration of practice, not just software

Products in this sector stay in service for decades. By the time one is replaced, it has accumulated a layer of institutional practice around it — workflows built on its quirks, local conventions, operators who can run it without reading the screen.

A replacement that is better in isolation can still fail, because the people who have to adopt it are being asked to give up competence they spent years acquiring. The design problem isn't only the destination. It's deciding what carries over, what gets a deliberate bridge, and what genuinely has to break — then sequencing the change so the teams depending on it are never standing on nothing.

## One language across a portfolio

Companies in this sector rarely ship one product. They ship a portfolio, often assembled across business units, programs, and countries, with teams that are separated by more than geography. Operators who move between those tools feel every seam.

A design system is the practical answer, but only when it's treated as shared infrastructure with real ownership rather than a style guide maintained on someone's margins. The ones that last are load-bearing: they outlive the team that wrote them because the products genuinely depend on them.
