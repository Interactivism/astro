## Designing for situational awareness

Situational awareness is knowing what is happening around you, what it means right now, and what it implies next. Human factors research separates it into three levels: **perception** of the critical elements, **comprehension** of what they mean together, and **projection** of where things are heading.

Most interfaces stop at the first. They display — accurately, completely, sometimes beautifully — and leave comprehension and projection entirely to the person watching. That holds up when there is time to think. It fails under workload and stress, which is precisely when the stakes are highest.

The design work is carrying more of that load: making the significant element distinguishable from the merely present, showing how elements relate rather than only listing them, and surfacing trajectory instead of just current state. The goal is an operator who stays in control and aware of the state of the system, rather than one reconstructing it from scratch under pressure.

## Modernization is a migration of practice, not just software

Ground systems and mission tooling stay in service for decades. By the time one is replaced, a layer of institutional practice has grown around it — workflows shaped by its quirks, local conventions, operators who can run it without reading the screen.

A replacement that is better in isolation can still fail, because the people being asked to adopt it are also being asked to give up competence they spent years acquiring. So the design problem is not only the destination. It is deciding what carries over, what needs a deliberate bridge, and what genuinely has to break — then sequencing the change so the teams depending on it are never standing on nothing.

## One language across a portfolio

Primes and space companies rarely ship a single product. They ship a portfolio, often assembled across business units, programs, and countries, with teams separated by more than geography. Operators who move between those tools feel every seam.

A design system is the practical answer, but only when it is treated as shared infrastructure with real ownership rather than a style guide maintained on someone's margins. The ones that last are load-bearing — they outlive the team that wrote them because the products genuinely depend on them.

## Most of this work stays behind the wall

Little of what we do here becomes a public case study. Programs are proprietary, the customer is often a government or a prime, and the details worth showing are usually the ones under NDA. That is ordinary for this category, but it is worth saying plainly rather than leaving you to draw conclusions from a short portfolio.

What we can show publicly is a fraction of what we have built. The rest we are glad to walk through directly, under NDA.
