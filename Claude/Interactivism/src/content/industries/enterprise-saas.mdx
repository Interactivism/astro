## The buyer and the user are different people

Procurement optimizes for the feature matrix. The person who opens your product every morning pays for whatever that matrix cost them — a settings screen with sixty fields because six customers each asked for ten, a workflow shaped by the demo rather than the job.

Both audiences are real and you have to serve both. But they need different things from the same product, and the sales conversation is louder than the daily one. Most of our enterprise work starts by getting the daily one back in the room.

## Legacy migration is a people problem wearing a technical costume

Replacing an entrenched enterprise product is rarely blocked by the new product being worse. It's blocked by a thousand accumulated habits, saved workflows, and muscle memory that the redesign quietly invalidates — and by the internal champion who has to defend the switch to colleagues who liked the old thing fine.

Modernization work that ignores this ships something demonstrably better that nobody adopts. Designing the transition — what carries over, what gets a bridge, what genuinely has to break — matters as much as designing the destination.

## Design systems are how enterprise portfolios stay coherent

Enterprise companies rarely have one product. They have a portfolio, often assembled through acquisition, each piece with its own conventions. Users move between them and feel every seam.

A design system is the practical answer, but only if it's built as shared infrastructure rather than a style guide someone maintains on the side. We've built and stewarded systems that span product lines and continents — the kind that survive team turnover because they're load-bearing, not decorative.
